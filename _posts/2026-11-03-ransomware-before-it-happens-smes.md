Most business owners think of ransomware as something that happens to large companies, hospitals or government departments. In reality, small and mid-sized businesses are attractive targets precisely because they tend to have fewer defences and less time to recover.

**Ransomware** is malicious software that locks or encrypts your files and systems, then demands payment to unlock them. Increasingly, attackers also copy your data before locking it and threaten to publish it if you do not pay.

The worst time to think about ransomware is the morning your staff cannot open a single file. The decisions that matter most are made long before that.

## Treat It as a Business Risk

The first shift is in mindset. Ransomware is not only a technical problem for your IT person or vendor to solve. It can stop billing, delay deliveries, expose customer data and damage your reputation.

That makes it a question for the owner or leadership team:

* How long could the business survive without its systems: a day, a week?
* Which systems would hurt most if they disappeared: accounts, sales, email, design files?
* Who decides what to do when it happens?

If nobody in the leadership team can answer these, that is the first gap to close.

## Make Sure You Can Recover Without Paying

The single most important protection against ransomware is a backup that the attacker cannot reach.

Many businesses do have backups, but they sit on a drive permanently connected to the same network, or in a shared folder that gets encrypted along with everything else. Modern ransomware actively looks for backups and destroys them first.

Ask your IT person or provider these plain questions:

* **Is at least one copy of our data offline or isolated,** so an attack on our network cannot touch it?
* **When did we last restore something from backup** to prove it actually works?
* **How long would a full recovery take,** and have we ever tested that?

A backup you have never restored is an assumption, not a safety net.

## Close the Usual Doors

Most ransomware does not arrive by magic. It typically gets in through a small number of familiar routes. Closing them removes much of the risk:

* **Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA)** for email, remote access and cloud accounts. A stolen password alone should not be enough to log in.
* **Keep systems updated.** Attackers regularly exploit known weaknesses in software that was never patched, including office devices such as network storage boxes and routers.
* **Lock down remote access.** Remote desktop connections open directly to the internet are a common entry point. Access should go through a secure, protected route.
* **Limit admin rights.** Everyday staff accounts should not be able to install software or change system settings.
* **Remove old accounts promptly** when people leave.

None of these steps require expensive products. They require discipline and someone accountable for making sure they are done.

## Prepare Your People

Many attacks begin with an email: a fake invoice, a courier notification or a message that looks like it is from a senior colleague.

Short, regular reminders work better than a single annual session. Staff should know what suspicious emails look like and, more importantly, feel safe reporting a mistake immediately. An employee who says "I think I clicked something" within minutes can save the business days of disruption.

## Write a One-Page Plan

You do not need a thick manual. A single page, printed and kept somewhere other than your computer systems, can make a huge difference. It should answer:

* **Who to call first:** your IT provider, with phone numbers that do not depend on company email.
* **Who makes decisions:** usually the owner or a named senior person.
* **What to do immediately:** disconnect affected machines from the network, but do not switch everything off or wipe devices before advice is taken.
* **How to keep operating:** manual processes for critical tasks such as billing or dispatch.
* **Who must be informed:** customers, your bank, your insurer if you have cyber cover, and the authorities. In India, CERT-In requires certain cyber incidents, including ransomware, to be reported within six hours of being noticed.

Walk through this plan once with your leadership team. Even a thirty-minute conversation will reveal assumptions that need fixing.

## Decide Your Position on Paying in Advance

Whether to pay a ransom is a difficult decision, and it is far better considered calmly than under pressure. Payment does not guarantee that your data will be returned, or that stolen data will not be published anyway. It may also mark your business as willing to pay.

The best way to avoid facing that choice is to make sure you can recover on your own.

## The Bigger Point

Ransomware succeeds when businesses are unprepared, not simply when attackers are clever. A tested backup, a few basic controls, alert staff and a one-page plan put an SME in a far stronger position than most.

**The time to prepare for ransomware is before it happens, because afterwards your options are few and all of them are expensive.**
