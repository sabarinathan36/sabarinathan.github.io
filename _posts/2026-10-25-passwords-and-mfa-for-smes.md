When business owners think about cybersecurity, they often picture expensive firewalls, specialist software and a team of experts.

In reality, a large share of security incidents begin with something far simpler: someone else logging in with a real employee's password.

The good news is that this is also one of the cheapest problems to fix. For most small and mid-sized businesses, improving passwords and switching on multi-factor authentication costs very little and delivers more protection than almost any other single step.

## Why Passwords Are the Weak Point

A password is the key to your business systems: email, accounting software, banking portals, cloud storage and customer records.

The trouble is how people actually use them:

* The same password is reused across many websites.
* Passwords are short, predictable or based on names and dates.
* Passwords are shared between colleagues "to save time".
* Former employees' accounts stay active long after they leave.

When any website suffers a data breach, the leaked email and password combinations are often tried against other services. If an employee reused their work password on that site, an attacker may simply walk in through the front door.

No alarm goes off, because from the system's point of view, it is a normal login.

## What MFA Actually Is

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**, sometimes called two-factor authentication or 2FA, means a login needs two things instead of one:

* Something you **know**, such as a password.
* Something you **have**, such as a code from an app on your phone.

Even if a criminal steals the password, they still cannot get in without the second factor.

You have almost certainly used this already. The one-time code your bank sends before a payment is a form of MFA. The same idea can protect your business email and other systems.

## Where to Switch It On First

You do not need to protect everything on day one. Start with the accounts that would hurt most if someone else controlled them:

1. **Email.** Your email account is the master key. Password resets for almost every other service are sent there.
2. **Banking and payment portals.** Anything that can move money.
3. **Accounting and payroll software.** Financial and employee data.
4. **Cloud file storage.** Where your contracts, proposals and customer files live.
5. **Administrator accounts.** Any account that can create users or change settings.

Most mainstream business platforms, including Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace, already include MFA in the subscription you are paying for. In many cases, it simply has not been switched on.

## Which Type of MFA to Choose

Not all second factors are equally strong. In plain terms:

* **Authenticator apps** (such as Microsoft Authenticator or Google Authenticator) are a good default for most staff. They are free and work on any smartphone.
* **SMS codes** are better than nothing, but text messages can be intercepted or redirected. Use them only where nothing else is offered.
* **Physical security keys** are small USB or tap devices. They offer the strongest protection and are worth considering for owners, finance staff and administrators.

A sensible approach is authenticator apps for everyone, and security keys for the handful of people with the most access.

## Fixing Passwords Without Annoying Everyone

Forcing staff to change complicated passwords every month usually backfires. People write them down or add "1" to the end each time.

A more practical approach:

* Encourage **long passphrases**, such as a few unrelated words joined together, rather than short complex passwords.
* Provide a **business password manager**, so staff do not need to remember dozens of passwords and nobody needs to share them in chat messages.
* **Never reuse** work passwords on personal websites.
* **Remove access the same day** someone leaves the business.

That last point is often overlooked. In my experience, the accounts of people who have left are among the most neglected risks in any organisation, large or small.

## Bringing Your Team Along

The technology is easy. The resistance usually comes from people who see MFA as an extra step.

A few things help:

* Explain the reason in business terms: it protects their own account and the company's money.
* Start with leadership. If the owner uses MFA, everyone else follows.
* Set a clear date and offer help on the day, rather than a vague request.
* Have a simple process for a lost or replaced phone, so nobody gets locked out.

## A Simple First Week Plan

If you do nothing else, ask whoever manages your IT to:

1. Switch on MFA for all email accounts.
2. Switch on MFA for banking, accounting and cloud storage.
3. Review the list of active user accounts and disable anyone who has left.
4. Choose a password manager for the team.

None of this requires a large budget. It requires a decision.

## Conclusion

Many businesses spend money on security tools while leaving their most valuable accounts protected by a single reused password.

Fixing that is quick, inexpensive and highly effective.

**Before you buy any new security product, make sure every important login needs more than a password.**
