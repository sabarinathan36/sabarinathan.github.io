## Background

ARTEX is an autonomous penetration testing framework written in the Go programming language that uses artificial intelligence agents to automate intrusion operations. According to BleepingComputer, the tool was initially published on GitHub in July 2026 by a developer known as Autumn-27, who removed the repository on 8 October 2026 after reports surfaced of its misuse. Despite the removal, the framework remains accessible through community forks, archived copies, localized versions, and a commercial variant named ARTEX PRO. ARTEX does not rely on a built-in model; instead, it integrates with external large language models (LLMs) including Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, DeepSeek, GLM, Grok, and Kimi to convert attacker instructions into automated execution commands.

## What happened

In a 13-page FLASH advisory published on a Friday, the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) disclosed that threat actors have been weaponising ARTEX since at least August 2026. The framework allows attackers with minimal technical skills to automate reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, system exploitation, privilege escalation, and data exfiltration across multiple targets simultaneously. The FBI stated that ARTEX drastically increases attack velocity, with some intrusions advancing from initial targeting to elevated system privileges in under three hours.

According to BleepingComputer, security researchers identified over 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles by early November. The advisory detailed several high-profile incidents involving the framework, including an October supply-chain breach of ConnectWise Cloud Backup (formerly SkyKick). Microsoft attributed that campaign to a China-aligned group tracked as Storm-0940, which used ARTEX to compromise application identities and attempt authentication against more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 customer environments, breaching at least 162 tenants.

ARTEX was also used alongside Claude Code agents in cyberattacks targeting South Korean financial institutions, including Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin Bank, and Hana Bank, exposing personal details of roughly 67,000 individuals. South Korean police subsequently arrested seven suspects connected to broader attacks on over 90 organisations that resulted in the theft of 8.2 million records. Additionally, cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike reported that a Chinese espionage group named Vapor Panda used ARTEX to target more than 200 organisations in 14 countries, compromising at least nine networks. To assist defenders, cybersecurity researcher MalwareMustDie released an open-source detection signature pack and playbook on Friday.

## Who is affected

According to BleepingComputer, the advisory affects organisations across the financial, government, technology, defence, and healthcare sectors globally. The framework is particularly adept at compromising internet-facing applications, partner portals, legacy services, employee applications, and cloud application permissions tied to managed service providers.

## Why it matters

The deployment of AI-driven tools like ARTEX lowers the technical barrier for cybercriminals, allowing low-skilled attackers to conduct complex, simultaneous operations at high speed. Because the framework can automatically chain attack techniques and adapt when initial execution attempts fail, IT defenders are left with a very narrow window to detect and stop breaches before administrative access is lost.

Furthermore, supply-chain incidents such as the ConnectWise breach demonstrate how a single compromised third-party integration can cascade into thousands of downstream customer cloud environments. The high volume of automated traffic generated by these tools can easily obscure malicious activity, placing critical corporate data and identities at severe risk.

## What to do now

1. Secure all internet-facing applications and restrict public access to cloud administration interfaces.
1. Review cloud application permissions, identity configurations, and authentication logs for unauthorized changes.
1. Enforce strict rate limits on sensitive application programming interfaces (APIs) to block automated attack bursts.
1. Monitor network and cloud infrastructure for unusual automated traffic, unexpected credential modifications, and large data transfers.
1. Implement specialized threat detection rules, such as the open-source ARTEX signature pack from MalwareMustDie, across security systems.

## Key takeaways

* ARTEX uses external LLMs to automate complex cyberattacks, enabling initial breaches in under three hours.
* Supply-chain integrations represent a primary target, exposing thousands of downstream cloud environments to automated intrusions.
* Defenders must focus on cloud identity governance, API rate limiting, and rapid automated threat detection to counter AI attack velocity.
